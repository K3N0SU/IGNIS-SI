## Project name

IGNIS-SI

## High-level summary

Fire in a spacecraft does not behave like fire on Earth. NASA's FLEX experiment on the International Space Station found that a burning fuel droplet can lose its visible flame and keep burning as a "cool flame" that a camera cannot see. IGNIS-SI turns eleven NASA microgravity combustion investigations into a working fire-safety dashboard. It summarises, compares and ranks the investigations for the situation you describe; rebuilds the droplet experiment as a physics model calibrated on 196 real ISS tests and checked on 65 more; shows the same fire through an optical camera and a UV sensor; predicts what happens when cabin fans are switched back on; recommends a life-support response; and always says whether NASA has actually tested the condition. An assistant answers questions in plain language by running the model and looking up the flight records.

## Detailed project description

**What it does.** IGNIS-SI has three views and an assistant.

- *Workbench.* Choose a fuel, droplet size, oxygen level, pressure and fan airflow. A 3D view shows the droplet, its flame and its soot shell, switchable between an optical camera and a UV sensor. Charts show droplet size, flame temperature, sensor signals and carbon monoxide, with the closest real NASA test drawn alongside. A plain-language panel states what the fire is doing and what each sensor can see. A response protocol lists life-support actions with oxygen, pressure and airflow targets. An evidence panel says whether the scenario is inside what NASA flew.
- *Flight data.* All 261 usable FLEX droplet burns plotted by size and oxygen; the model plotted against flight measurements; calibration results on held-out tests; and NASA's investigations compared by fuel, oxygen, airflow and sensors and ranked for the current scenario, each with its findings and DOI.
- *Analytics.* Cabin materials ranked across the ISS, 10.2 psia and Artemis atmospheres; a map of tested and untested conditions; how fire intensity responds to airflow; how smoke particles grow in orbit and what that does to detectors.
- *Assistant.* A tool-using AI agent. It can run a simulation, fetch the nearest flight tests, compare investigations, read the risk ranking and load a scenario into the workbench. It is instructed never to state a number that did not come from a tool, and to say whether a statement is a NASA measurement or a model prediction.

**How it works.** The backend is Python (FastAPI, NumPy, SciPy, scikit-learn). The physics is a reduced-order droplet combustion model: the d-squared law with Stefan flow and gas radiation, a Damkohler-number extinction test, a low-temperature cool-flame branch, a model of slow airflow, water absorption for methanol, and a sectional smoke coagulation model. Nine constants were fitted to 196 FLEX flight tests; on 65 held-out tests the burning-rate error is 18 % and 76 % of extinction diameters are reproduced within 0.2 of the initial diameter. A gradient-boosted classifier identifies the fire state from radiometer, UV and gas sensors without camera input. The frontend is Next.js with three.js and Recharts.

**Benefits.** Crews and designers get a way to see a hazard that standard optical checks miss, to test a response before using it, and to know when a prediction goes beyond flight evidence. Researchers get NASA's combustion investigations in one comparable, ranked view with their sources.

**What it is not.** Time series on screen are simulated; NASA's tables give each test's conditions and outcomes, not second-by-second telemetry. The fan-restart re-ignition is a model prediction that no orbital droplet test covers; the app marks it "Beyond frontier". The material ranking uses handbook constants and is indicative.

## Use of artificial intelligence

State this in the submission form; Space Apps asks teams to disclose AI use.

- **In the product:** the assistant uses Google Gemini through its API. A scikit-learn classifier, trained on the project's own simulations, identifies the fire state.
- **In building the project:** an AI coding assistant (Anthropic's Claude, through Claude Code) wrote most of the code, documentation and film scripts under the team's direction, and ran the tests and calibration.
- **Media:** the film soundtrack was synthesised by a script in this repository (`scripts/make_score.py`), not by a generative-music service. No AI-generated images or NASA logos are used. Captions and title cards in the films are drawn by the recording script.

## Self-assessment against the judging criteria

Each criterion is scored 1 to 5 by judges. This is the team's own honest reading, not a prediction.

| Criterion | Strengths | Weaknesses a judge may raise |
|---|---|---|
| **Impact** | A real hazard (a fire that looks out and is not) with a concrete, numeric response; relevant to Artemis cabin atmospheres | A decision aid that has not been reviewed by flight operations; single-droplet physics is far from a real cabin fire |
| **Creativity** | Same fire through two sensors; "has NASA tested this?" as a first-class answer with the nearest real tests; an assistant that operates the dashboard | The dashboard format itself is what the challenge suggested |
| **Validity** | Calibrated on real flight data with a held-out check; 107 unit tests plus browser and load tests; every source cited by DOI; limits stated in the interface | 18 % burning-rate error; methanol near its flammability limit is weak; re-ignition under airflow has no flight data; built in a weekend |
| **Relevance** | Uses the exact repository the challenge names; summarises, ranks, explains and compares investigations by the attributes it lists | Saffire IV to VI and ACME tables were not included; investigations are ranked by a simple transparent score, not a learned one |
| **Presentation** | Plain-language interface, guided tour, 30-second film and five-minute walkthrough, one-command start, full README | Desktop only; soundtrack unreviewed; the films replay a pre-fetched assistant answer to save time (disclosed) |
